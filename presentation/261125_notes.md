## 261125a: slide 1, "Gusts: what we will implement"

**The point:** the solver can already fly through any wind. All that's missing is a
believable gust history. Four steps produce one.

1. **Dryden:** a measured description of turbulence. Two inputs (mean wind at 20 ft,
   height) give two numbers per direction: how strong the gusts are (σ, a standard
   deviation in kt) and how big they are (L, a length in ft).
2. **Fly through it:** picture the gusts as a pattern frozen in the air. At speed V you
   cross one gust of length L in T = L/V seconds, so T is how long a gust lasts.
3. **An SDE:** an equation for a random signal that fades over T, gets kicked by noise,
   and keeps a typical size σ.
4. **Step it:** turn that equation into a loop that produces a number every 0.01 s.

**The plot:** that loop's output. The shaded band is ±σ: the gust spends about
two-thirds of its time inside it. It changes over about half a second (T = 0.57 s).

## 261125a: slide 2, "Then: many gusty takeoffs"

* **Fly:** the gust adds to the airspeed and changes the angle of attack. The crew
  doesn't know the gusts are coming, so they fly the calm-day V1 and VR. They react to
  the airspeed about 1 s late, like a real pilot reading an instrument.
* **Repeat:** 500 takeoffs give 500 runway lengths. Sort them: the 475th is the length
  that covers 95 % of gusty days.
* **Check:** four tests that the numbers are right. Slides 7, 8 and 11 of the full deck
  show them done.

## Full deck

### 2. The only new ingredient: a gust history
* The two equations are where the gust enters. u_g is the along-runway gust (positive is
  a headwind) and w_g the vertical one (positive is up).
* Nothing else in the solver changes. Once we have a gust history, solving the takeoff is
  the same problem as before. **The real work is making the gust history.**
* **Plot:** three takeoffs in the same weather, each with its own gusts. You can't
  predict which one you'll get, which is why we run hundreds.
* Worth pointing at: the gusts look calmer early in the roll and busier later. That's
  slide 4 in action: faster aircraft, shorter T.

### 3. How strong and how big: Dryden
* Hugh Dryden led NACA, NASA's predecessor. The low-altitude form we use was
  standardized in a 1980 military spec, MIL-F-8785C, and was fitted to measurements of
  real turbulence.
* **W20 is the mean wind speed at 20 ft.** The turbulence level picks it: light 15,
  moderate 30, severe 45 kt. σ_w = 0.1·W20 says vertical gusts are typically 10 % of the
  mean wind.
* **Plot, left ("how strong"):** gust strength against height. Vertical strength is the
  same at every height; along-runway gusts are stronger near the ground.
* **Plot, right ("how big"):** gust length against height. Gusts are small near the
  ground and grow with height.
* **The honest caveat (bottom band):** we live in the bottom 50 ft, but most of the
  measurements were taken at 100–1,000 ft, so down here the formulas are extrapolated.
  On the runway we hold h at 10 ft, which is roughly wing height.
* **If asked "is this for real flight?":** yes. It's the standard model for simulating
  takeoff and landing. The extrapolation near the ground is a known weak spot, and we
  list it in the limits.

### 4. From size to time: T = L / V
* Turbulence is a pattern in **space**. Our solver runs in **time**. The bridge is to
  treat the pattern as frozen (it moves much slower than the aircraft), so flying
  through it at V turns a length into a time.
* **Plot ①:** one frozen pattern, with distance on the x-axis. The arrow shows L, a
  typical gust size. The bars show how much of the pattern each aircraft crosses in 4 s.
* **Plots ② and ③:** what each aircraft feels over those 4 s. At 150 kt it races through
  the pattern, so the gust changes every ~0.6 s. At 40 kt it crawls, so the gust changes
  every ~2 s. Same air, different speed, different memory.
* **Why it matters:** V changes during the takeoff, so T changes too. We recompute it
  every step.

### 5. Building the equation: a memory plus random kicks
Build the update rule one term at a time. All three panels use the same random numbers.
* **Pull only:** each step removes a fraction Δt/T of the gust. Start at 14 kt and it
  fades away exponentially, like e^(−t/T). That's the memory, but nothing keeps it going.
* **Kicks only:** add a random number every step. This is a random walk: it drifts off
  and never settles, so its size keeps growing.
* **Both:** the pull drags big gusts back and the kicks keep it alive, so it settles at
  a steady size (the band) and changes over about T.
* This is the **Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process**, a well-known SDE. The next slide finds c.

### 6. Where σ√(2/T) and √Δt come from
* **Left box (the variance balance):** square the update rule and take the average. The
  kick Z_n is new, so it's independent of g_n, and their variances just add. For a
  steady gust the variance must stay the same from step to step. Solve for c and you get
  c = σ√(2/T).
* That's all "the math behind the noise term" is: the kick has to exactly refill what
  the pull removes.
* **Right box (why √Δt):** independent kicks add their variances. Halve Δt and you get
  twice as many kicks, so each must have half the variance, which means a kick of size
  √Δt.
* **Plot:** with kicks ∝ Δt, the sum of the kicks shrinks to nothing as Δt → 0: the
  randomness disappears when you refine the step. With kicks ∝ √Δt, every Δt gives the
  same spread. That's Brownian motion.
* **Bottom:** the equation's correlation is e^(−|τ|/T). Its Fourier transform is
  exactly Dryden's along-runway spectrum. **So this equation isn't a guess that looks
  like Dryden; it *is* the Dryden model, written so we can step it in time.**
* **If asked about the vertical gust:** Dryden's vertical spectrum has a slightly
  different shape. We use the same equation for it (an approximation, listed in the
  limits).

### 7. Stepping it: Euler–Maruyama vs Heun
* **Euler–Maruyama** is forward Euler plus the kick.
* **Heun** first takes an Euler–Maruyama step as a guess, then redoes the step with the
  average of the two slopes, using the same random kick. It's the stochastic version of
  the trapezoid / improved Euler method.
* For this simple equation we can compute exactly what variance each method produces
  (the left-box algebra from slide 6, keeping every term):
  * EM: σ²/(1 − Δt/2T), too big by about Δt/2T. That's first order.
  * Heun: off by only about (Δt/T)²/4. That's second order.
* **Plot:** both axes are logarithmic. The x-axis is the time step measured in units of
  the gust's memory. A slope of 1 means halving Δt halves the error; a slope of 2 means
  halving Δt quarters it. The lines are the exact formulas; the dots come from
  simulation and land right on them. The shaded band is where our takeoffs operate.
* **Takeaway:** at 0.01 s, EM's error is 1.7 % at worst and Heun's is 0.03 %. That's why
  Heun is the default.

### 8. Check: the generated gusts match Dryden
* Hold the height and speed fixed, run the generator for a long time, and measure what
  it produced.
* **Plot ①:** a histogram of the gust values (divided by σ), against a normal curve with
  standard deviation σ. Measured: 0.995 σ.
* **Plot ②:** correlation means "how similar is the gust now to the gust τ seconds
  later?" It starts at 1 (identical) and decays like e^(−τ/T). After one T, 37 % is
  left. The measured curve (blue) sits under the target (dashed).
* **Takeaway:** the generator produces exactly what Dryden asks for. This check also
  runs in the code's test suite.

### 9. The small part: fly the calm-day schedule through the gusts
* The gusts become two extra rows in the state vector, and everything is stepped
  together.
* **Plot:** the last 24 s of one takeoff. Grey is the true airspeed the wing feels, with
  every gust on it. Black is what the crew reacts to: the gust part arrives 1 s late, so
  the line is smoother. The engine failure and rotation are triggered when the black line
  crosses the calm-day speeds. Note the spike at 50 s: the grey line touches VR but the
  crew doesn't rotate yet.
* **Span averaging:** a 196 ft wing can't feel a 10 ft gust as a whole. Half the wing
  goes up while the other half goes down, and they cancel. So the wing feels gusts
  averaged over its span.
* **Sanity check:** with the gusts switched off, this stepped simulation matches the main
  (adaptive) solver.

### 10. Many runs → a runway with a confidence
* **Plot:** pick how many gusty days you want to cover on the x-axis (95 % means 19 days
  in 20), then read how much more runway than the calm-day answer that takes. One line
  per turbulence level. The x-axis is stretched toward 99 %, so the rare days get room.
* **P95 = the 950th of 1,000 sorted runs.** Its uncertainty comes from counting: how
  many runs fall below the true P95 is a binomial count, and that gives the interval
  (the shaded band) without assuming any distribution.
* **Table:** the SR-71 is far more gust-sensitive. With one engine out it has little
  thrust to spare, so a bad gust near lift-off costs it a lot of distance.

### 11. Error budget: the number of runs matters, the time step doesn't
* **Plot:** three numbers on one scale. The effect we're measuring (+394 ft), the
  uncertainty from using only 500 runs (±45 ft), and the error from the time step (±4 ft).
* **Table:** the same gusts solved at four step sizes, compared with a very fine one. The
  errors shrink roughly in proportion to Δt, but unevenly. That's because events like
  rotation and braking happen between steps, and locating them is only first order.
* **Takeaway (the numerical-analysis point):** spend effort where the error is. A smaller
  Δt would fix 4 ft; 4× the runs would halve the 45 ft.

### 12. Summary and limits
* The pipeline in one line, the answer, and three honest limits: the vertical-gust
  approximation, extrapolation near the ground, and a simple pilot and steady mean wind.

## Likely questions

* **Why not use the exact solution of the OU equation instead of stepping it?** For
  fixed T there's an exact update: g ← e^(−Δt/T) g + σ√(1 − e^(−2Δt/T)) Z. But in the
  simulation T changes every step with the speed and height, and the gust is stepped
  together with the aircraft equations. A general SDE method (EM or Heun) handles the
  whole system the same way.
* **Is the noise Itô or Stratonovich?** The noise strength depends on height and speed,
  not on the gust itself, so the two interpretations agree. EM and Heun converge to the
  same answer.
* **Why does the rest of the solver use RK45 but not this part?** High-order ODE methods
  rely on a smooth right-hand side. Brownian kicks are continuous but nowhere
  differentiable, so that order is lost.
* **Isn't P95 just mean + 2σ?** Only for a normal distribution. We read it straight off
  the sorted runs, so no shape is assumed.
* **Why 500 runs?** It's where the sampling error (±45 ft) is small next to the effect
  (+394 ft). 2,000 runs would give about ±21 ft.
