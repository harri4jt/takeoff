One running example appears everywhere, so the numbers connect from slide to slide:
**moderate turbulence, 20 ft above the runway: along-runway σ_u = 5.8 kt, L_u = 144 ft;
vertical σ_w = 3.0 kt, L_w = 20 ft.** At 150 kt the along-runway gust lasts T = 0.57 s;
at 40 kt, T = 2.1 s.

Notation: **u** is along the runway (positive is a headwind gust), **w** is vertical
(positive is an updraft). The usual aero letters for x, y, z; v, sideways, isn't
modelled.

## 261125a (3 slides)

### 1. Gust model
* **Top line:** the general form of any SDE. Drift (blue) is the ordinary ODE part;
  diffusion (orange) is the random part, driven by Brownian motion dW.
* **Big line:** our gust equation in exactly that form. g stands for u_g or w_g: one
  equation each, with their own σ and L.
  * The drift −(g/T) pulls the gust back to zero, and T sets how fast.
  * The diffusion σ√(2/T) dW kicks it randomly, and that coefficient keeps its size at σ.
* **The three boxes** say where each symbol comes from: σ and L from Dryden, T = L/V
  (how long the aircraft takes to fly through one gust at its current speed), and dW
  from Brownian motion, √Δt · Z per step.
* **Plot:** what the update rule produces, a value every 0.01 s. The shaded band is ±σ;
  the gust is inside it about two-thirds of the time.

### 2. Gusts in the equations of motion
* The gusts enter only through the air the wing sees: the airspeed, and the angle of
  attack (an updraft raises it).
* **Ground roll:** same as before, with u_g added to the airspeed.
* **Airborne:** Newton's law for the velocity relative to the ground. Lift and drag are
  computed from the velocity relative to the air, which contains the gusts.
* **The key point:** the 11/20 equations were written relative to the air and needed
  the wind's derivative. A random gust has no derivative, because Brownian motion is
  continuous but nowhere differentiable. Written for the ground velocity, only the gust's
  value appears.
* RK4/RK45 get their order from a smooth right-hand side. With noise in it that's gone,
  so we use SDE methods (Euler–Maruyama, Heun).

### 3. Then: many gusty takeoffs
* 500 takeoffs on the calm-day schedule, each with new random gusts. Sort the 500
  runway lengths: the 475th covers 95 % of gusty days.
* The checks say how we'll know the numbers are right. Slides 6 and 8 of the full deck
  show them done.

## 261125 (10 slides)

### 2. Updating the solver: wind as a random input
* The two equations show where a gust enters.
* **Randomness:** each time step the gust gets a random increment (a Brownian step), so
  the takeoff ODE becomes an SDE.
* **Monte Carlo** (estimating something by repeated random sampling): each run gives one runway length. With many runs, the share that fit
  a given runway is the probability that runway is enough.
* **Plot:** three takeoffs in the same weather, each with its own gusts. The gusts look
  slower early in the roll and busier later, because a faster aircraft gives a shorter T.

### 3. Gust intensity and scale length: Dryden
* **Inputs → outputs.** W20 is the mean wind at 20 ft (where airport wind sensors sit);
  h is the height.
* **Is σ a standard deviation?** Yes. The gust speed is random with mean 0, and σ is
  its standard deviation, so the gust is within ±σ about 68 % of the time.
  "Intensity" and "scale length" are the turbulence terms the spec itself uses.
* **Say out loud:**
  * σ_w = 0.1 W20: near the ground, turbulence comes from wind rubbing on the ground, so
    it scales with the wind. The 0.1 is measured.
  * L_w = h: the ground blocks vertical motion, so a vertical eddy can't be taller than
    its height.
  * The bracket (0.177 + 0.000823 h) equals 1 at 1,000 ft. There u and w become equal
    (the ground no longer matters). Near the ground the along-runway gusts are about 2×
    stronger and 7× longer.
* We pick one of the spec's three levels: light 15, moderate 30, severe 45 kt.
* On the runway h is held at 10 ft. The formulas come from data mostly above 100 ft, so
  near the ground they're extrapolated (it's in the limits).

### 4. The gust SDE: drift plus diffusion
* **T** in one sentence: the time the aircraft, at its current speed, takes to pass
  through one gust.
* Same random kicks in all three panels:
  * **Drift only:** dg = −(g/T) dt is an ordinary ODE with solution g₀e^(−t/T). Start at
    14 kt and it dies out.
  * **Diffusion only:** dg = c dW is Brownian motion. Its variance grows like c²t, so
    it wanders off and never settles.
  * The drift-only panel shows one 14 kt gust with no kicks after it: how a single kick fades.
  * **Drift + diffusion:** not the sum of the first two curves. Every kick, from the
    moment it happens, decays like e^(−age/T). The gust is the sum of all recent kicks,
    each faded by its age. Old kicks are forgotten, so the variance stops growing and
    settles at σ².
* **Box:** drift gives the memory, diffusion gives the variance, and together they make
  a stationary process: same statistics at every time. Named the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck SDE.

### 5. Deriving the SDE: the kick size c that keeps Var = σ²
* **Is the equation made up?** No. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is a standard model
  (Ornstein and Uhlenbeck, 1930), and it's the usual way simulators generate Dryden's
  along-runway gust. This slide shows why its kick coefficient is σ√(2/T).
* **Which method is box ①?** Euler–Maruyama: one step of it, then the requirement that
  the variance stays at σ². That's simply the easiest way to see the balance.
  * The pull shrinks the variance by (1 − Δt/T)², and the kick adds c²Δt.
  * For the variance to stay at σ², c² = 2σ²/T.
  * Keep the leftover Δt² term and you get Euler–Maruyama's own error, the next slide.
* **Where √Δt appears:** in step ①, dW is replaced by ΔW = √Δt · Z_n, so the kick is c√Δt Z_n.
* **Right box:** halve Δt and you get twice as many kicks. Independent kicks add variances,
  so each kick needs half the variance, which means its size goes like √Δt. The plot
  shows it: with kicks ∝ Δt the randomness shrinks to nothing as Δt → 0.
* **Bottom right:** the SDE's correlation, Fourier-transformed, is Dryden's along-runway
  spectrum, so the SDE is the Dryden model in a form we can step.

### 6. Time stepping: Euler–Maruyama vs Heun
* **Euler–Maruyama** is forward Euler plus the Brownian kick.
* **Heun is not centred differencing.** It's the explicit trapezoidal rule, also called
  improved Euler, a 2nd-order Runge–Kutta:
  1. Take a forward-Euler step to guess the end value g̃.
  2. Redo the step with the average of the slopes at the start (g_n) and the guess (g̃).
  3. Use the same random kick both times.
  The midpoint method is the other common RK2; that one evaluates the slope at half a
  step. Neither is centred differencing, which uses points on both sides of n.
* **What the graph is:** both methods run the gust equation for a long time, then we ask
  how far the variance of the gusts they produce is from the true σ².
  * x-axis: the step size divided by the gust's memory, Δt/T. Small means many steps
    per gust.
  * y-axis: that variance error in %.
  * Both axes are logarithmic, so the slope of a line is the method's order. Slope 1
    (Euler–Maruyama) means halving Δt halves the error. Slope 2 (Heun) means halving Δt
    quarters it.
  * Lines: exact formulas (Euler–Maruyama σ²/(1 − Δt/2T); Heun off by about
    (Δt/T)²/4). Dots: measured from simulation, landing on the lines.
  * Shaded: where our takeoffs operate.
* **Where it's used:** every gusty run steps the aircraft and the gusts together with Heun at
  Δt = 0.01 s. RK45 is still used for the calm-day takeoff, which sets V1 and VR.
* **Takeaway:** at 0.01 s, Euler–Maruyama inflates the gust variance by up to 1.7 % and
  Heun by 0.03 %.

### 7. Monte Carlo: many gusty takeoffs → runway vs confidence
* The bullets say how each run is flown. P95 is read straight off the sorted runs, with
  no distribution assumed. Its confidence interval comes from counting: how many runs
  fall below the true P95 is a binomial count.
* **Plot:** pick a share of gusty days on the x-axis (95 % means 19 days in 20) and read
  the extra runway over calm. One line per turbulence level. The band widens to the right
  because fewer runs lie beyond a high percentile: P99 of 1,000 runs rests on the ~10
  most extreme ones.
* **Table:** the SR-71 is far more sensitive. With one engine out it has little thrust to
  spare, so a bad gust near lift-off costs it a lot.

### 8. Error 1, sampling
* **Left plot:** one 500-run study. P95 is the 475th sorted run, +365 ft.
* **The interval:** the count of runs below the true P95 is Binomial(500, 0.95), with mean
  475 and standard deviation 4.9. So the true P95 sits between runs 465 and 485 (95 %
  confidence): +337 to +418 ft. No distribution is assumed for the runway itself.
* **Right plot:** the check. Redo the study 400 times and see how much P95 moves: ±47 ft
  at 500 runs. It shrinks like 1/√N (±86, ±47, ±21 for 125, 500, 2,000 runs).

### 9. Error 2, time step
* **The trick:** one Brownian path on a fine grid. A coarse step's increment is the sum of
  the fine ones, so every Δt flies through the same gusts. The difference between step
  sizes is then purely time-step error (strong error), not different luck.
* e(Δt) is the mean over 16 paths of |distance at Δt − distance at the reference step|.
* **Plot:** roughly slope 1. Stop is the noisiest, because braking and idle switch at
  fixed times and fall between steps.
* **Comparison:** at 0.01 s the time step costs ≤ 4 ft, against ±47 ft sampling and a
  +394 ft effect. More runs, not a smaller step.

### 10. Summary and limits
* The method as a table, the answer with both error sizes, and three limits:
  * the vertical-gust approximation (Dryden's vertical spectrum isn't exactly the
    Ornstein–Uhlenbeck one);
  * extrapolation near the ground;
  * a simple pilot and a steady mean wind.

## Likely questions

* **Why not make the gust history up front?** The random kicks are drawn up front. The
  gust itself depends on T = L/V, and V depends on the gust, so the two are solved
  together, one step at a time.
* **Why not the exact Ornstein–Uhlenbeck update?** For a fixed T there is one:
  g ← e^(−Δt/T) g + σ√(1 − e^(−2Δt/T)) Z. But in a run T changes every step, and the gust
  is stepped together with the aircraft, so a general SDE method handles the whole
  system.
* **Itô or Stratonovich?** The noise strength depends on height and speed, not on the
  gust, so the two agree. Euler–Maruyama and Heun converge to the same answer.
* **Isn't P95 just mean + 2σ?** Only for a normal distribution. We read it off the
  sorted runs, so no shape is assumed.
* **Why 500 runs?** The sampling error (±47 ft) is small next to the effect (+394 ft).
  2,000 runs would give about ±21 ft.
