Balanced Field Length:
gust analysis

How much extra runway does a gusty day need, and how sure can we be of that number?
John Harrison & Connor Seng

Why a gust analysis?

  • The balanced field length is computed for a calm day: steady wind.
  • Real days are gusty. The crew still flies the schedule set before takeoff (V1, VR from the calm-day answer), but gusts move where on the runway those speeds are reached, and change lift and drag on the way to 35 ft.
  • So the runway actually used varies from takeoff to takeoff.
  • The question: how big a cushion over the calm-day answer covers 95 % (or 99 %) of gusty-day takeoffs?
  • Not a worst case: a carefully chosen gust can beat any runway. It is a probability: how often a given runway is enough.

Bottom line: on a moderately gusty day, about 400 ft (4 %) more runway than the calm-day answer covers 95 % of 747 takeoffs; covering 99 % takes about 560 ft (6 %).

The Dryden turbulence model: what the gusts are built on

Where it comes from

A statistical description of real atmospheric turbulence, fitted to measurements and named after Hugh Dryden (NACA). Standardized for flight simulation by the military flying-qualities spec MIL-F-8785C (1980), carried on in MIL-HDBK-1797. We use the low-altitude form (below 1,000 ft). Chosen over von Kármán because its gusts can be made by filtering random numbers: what our update rule does.

What it prescribes (and what it does not)

Only the statistics of the gusts, not a particular gust history. For each direction (along the runway u, vertical w): σ, how strong a typical gust is; L, how long a typical gust is in space; and the spectrum, how the gust energy is spread over wavelengths. The air is treated as frozen and the aircraft flies through it, so its speed V turns a length into a time, T = L / V.
Inputs: W20 and h. W20: wind speed at 20 ft; h: aircraft height in ft (held at ≥ 10 ft on the runway).
σw = 0.1 · W20 vertical strength
σu = σw / (0.177 + 0.000823 h)0.4 along-runway strength
Lw = h vertical length
Lu = h / (0.177 + 0.000823 h)1.2 along-runway length

Known approximation: with Lw = h/2, which matches Dryden's slow content, the moderate P95 is unchanged for the 747 (+380 vs +376 ft, 400 runs) and about 10 % lower for the SR-71 (+985 vs +1,093 ft).

How a gust curve is made

1
From Dryden (previous slide), at the current height: a strength σ and a length L for each gust direction. Moderate: σw 3 kt, σu ≈ 6 kt; Lu ~75–300 ft, Lw = h.
2
Memory T = L / airspeed: the time to fly through one gust. A 140-ft gust lasts 2 s at 40 kt, 0.5 s at 150 kt.
3
Every 0.01 s step:
gnew = gold − (gold / T)·Δt + σ √(2/T) · √Δt · Z
pull back toward 0 (the memory) + a random kick, Z a standard normal random number. Result: a curve that wanders around 0 with typical size σ and changes over about T seconds (an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process; the kicks add up to Brownian motion). The rule piece by piece: next slide.
4
Drawn up front, built as you go: the Z's for a run come from a random seed before it starts (same seed → same run); the curve is built step by step because σ, L and T depend on the aircraft's current height and speed.
Same random numbers, two memories: a fast aircraft (short T) sees a jumpier gust. Why √Δt in the kick: random kicks add like a random walk, so this scaling keeps the gusts the same whatever the time step.

The update rule, piece by piece

gnew = goldwhere the gust is now − (gold / T)·Δtthe pull back toward 0 + σ √(2/T) · √Δt · Zthe random kick

The pull alone: exponential, not linear

Each step removes the fraction Δt/T of whatever is there now: a big gust is pulled back hard, a small one gently. As Δt → 0 this is dg/dt = −g/T, so g = g₀ e−t/T: 37 % left after T, 14 % after 2T, 5 % after 3T. T is the gust's memory. (Dots on ①: the rule step by step, Δt = 0.1 s.)

"What does one gust look like?"

In this model a single gust is one kick: a sudden jump, then an exponential fade (①). The real gust is all the recent kicks added up, each faded by e−age/T (②): only the last few T seconds matter. A gust that stays constant and then stops (a step, or the "1 − cos" shape) is a different model, the discrete gust used for structural loads; it is not used here.

The kick, and why those factors

A new random jump every step, Z a standard normal number. √Δt: kicks add like a random walk (their variances add), so the gusts come out the same whatever the step. √(2/T): each step the pull removes ≈ 2Δt/T of the variance and the kick adds σ²·2Δt/T; they balance when Var = σ². So the gust's typical size is σ, whatever T.

From gusts to a takeoff: a fixed schedule, read with a lag

1
The calm-day solve fixes the schedule: engine-failure speed VEF 141 kt, V1 (1 s later), VR 154 kt.
2
Each run: the engine fails when the crew's airspeed reaches VEF; from that moment both the go and the stop are flown, through the same gusts.
3
No re-balance: nobody knows the gusts in advance. The runway that run needs is the larger of go, stop and 1.15 × the all-engines takeoff.
4
Pilot lag: events fire on true airspeed with its gust part smoothed over ~1 s (pilot and instrument response), so a gust spike that touches VR does not start the rotation (right).
Q: real airspeed readings lag too, so why lag only the gust part?
They do lag a little (pressure tubing, display filtering, reaction). A lagged reading behaves like a smoothed speedometer: it tracks a steady acceleration almost exactly, just behind by (acceleration × lag) ≈ 1.5–4 kt/s × 1 s, and averages out fast wiggles. That small steady offset is the same in the calm and the gusty runs and is part of how the calm schedule is flown; the answer here is gusty minus calm from the same simulator. Lagging only the gust part isolates what gusts change; lagging everything would shift both runs by about the same amount.

What a gust physically is, and why the wing averages small ones out

  • A gust is an eddy: a swirl of air carried along by the wind. Flying through one, the air suddenly comes faster or slower (horizontal gust) or pushes up or down (vertical gust).
  • Why "wind at 20 ft": near the ground, turbulence is made by wind rubbing over rough ground; the stronger the wind, the stronger the eddies. The standard measures that wind 20 ft up, a typical mast height.
  • Why a gust has a length: eddies have a size, and near the ground they cannot be bigger than their height: vertical ones are ~10 ft at the wheels, ~50 ft at the screen height.
  • Why T depends on the takeoff: the eddy pattern drifts with the wind and barely changes in the few seconds a jet takes to cross it, so the faster you go, the faster each gust comes and goes.
The wing feels the gust averaged over its span. A 196-ft 747 wing in 10-ft vertical eddies: the moderate 3.0 kt gusts average to 0.9 kt (1.9 kt at 50 ft). Without this, ultra-short gusts made the simulated jet skip on and off the runway, which a real wing does not do. All it needs is the wingspan.

Sampling error: how sure is the P95?

What it is

Rerun the 500-run study with another random seed and the P95 comes out a little different. Sampling error is how much it moves from one repeat to the next (right: 400 repeats). Left uses a different 500 runs than slide 2: +365 here vs +394 there, that difference is sampling error.

The interval from one study

Each run lands below the true P95 with probability 0.95, like a weighted coin. In 500 runs: 475 ± √(500 · 0.95 · 0.05) ≈ 475 ± 5 below it. So 95 % of the time the true P95 is between the 465th and 485th sorted runs: +337 … +418 ft (left, orange).

Why 4× the runs halves it

The count's spread grows like √N, but the runs pack N times closer, so the width shrinks like 1/√N: ±86 → ±47 → ±21 ft for 125 → 500 → 2,000 runs. The time-step error at Δt = 0.01 s is only a few feet, so accuracy comes from more runs, not smaller steps.
